# Literature Review: Sources Cited in Vignette Design & Fault-Rating Scale

This review compiles every source referenced over the course of designing the gender-bias dispute-arbitration vignette study — covering (1) the taxonomy of relational obligation sources used to construct vignette premises, and (2) the theoretical grounding for the fault-rating scale used as the dependent variable. Sources are organized by the design question they were pulled in to answer, not strictly by citation order.

---

## 1. Sources of Relational Obligation

These informed the `obligation_source` taxonomy used across the 9 vignette families — the menu of reasons a vignette can give for *why* the agent had a responsibility to the partner in the first place.

### Explicit / negotiated obligation

**Scanlon, T.M. (1998).** *What We Owe to Each Other.* Harvard University Press.
Scanlon's treatment of promising (Ch. 7) and of assurance (Principle F) is the source for two related but distinct obligation types in the design: the idea that a promise or agreement is self-binding independent of any further justification, and — more precisely applicable to the vignettes — that intentionally inducing or knowingly disregarding a partner's *reasonable reliance* on a stated fact carries independent moral weight, separate from promising itself.

**Hardimon, Michael O. (1994).** "Role Obligations." *Journal of Philosophy*, 91(7), 333–363.
The standard reference in analytic ethics for role-based duties. Distinguishes obligations that come from role *membership* (unchosen, e.g. being a parent) from role *assignment* (a specific division of labor within a role, which can be chosen or negotiated). The vignette design uses the assignment sense — marriage or partnership alone doesn't determine who does a given task; a specific assignment does.

*(A proposed contemporary counterpoint to Hardimon — Brake (2010) on voluntarist role obligations — has been moved to `unverified_sources.md`, since it hasn't been independently confirmed.)*

### Emergent / tacit obligation

**Gouldner, Alvin W. (1960).** "The Norm of Reciprocity: A Preliminary Statement." *American Sociological Review*, 25(2), 161–178.
Foundational sociological source treating reciprocity as a generalized, largely unspoken background norm rather than a specific negotiated exchange. Used to ground obligation types where nothing was ever explicitly agreed, but an expectation of reciprocal contribution has built up over time.

**Daminger, Allison (2019).** "The Cognitive Dimension of Household Labor." *American Sociological Review*, 84(4), 609–633.
The academic origin of the "mental load" concept, decomposed into anticipating, deciding, and monitoring labor. Directly relevant to any vignette family involving mental load or invisible household coordination — its core empirical claim (that this labor is often unspoken and unassigned rather than explicitly divided) is the basis for treating "established joint practice" as its own obligation category, distinct from an explicit agreement.

**Hochschild, Arlie (1983).** *The Managed Heart: Commercialization of Human Feeling.* University of California Press.
**Hochschild, Arlie (1989).** *The Second Shift: Working Parents and the Revolution at Home.* Viking.
Origin of the term "emotional labor" and empirical documentation of unequal domestic labor distribution. Sociological/empirical rather than normative-ethical in character — useful as background for why certain norm families (household labor, emotional labor) are worth studying at all, less useful as a source for the obligation taxonomy itself.

### Structural / communal obligation

**Clark, Margaret S., & Mills, Judson (1979).** "Interpersonal Attraction in Exchange and Communal Relationships." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 37(1), 12–24.
**Clark, Margaret S., & Mills, Judson (1993).** "The Difference between Communal and Exchange Relationships: What It Is and Is Not." *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 19(6), 684–691.
The standard citation for the distinction between communal relationships (help given based on need, not tracked reciprocity) and exchange relationships (tracked, transactional reciprocity). This is social psychology, not moral philosophy — it grounds the *empirical* claim that intimate relationships are expected to run on need-responsiveness, not a normative argument for why that expectation is justified.

**Fiske, Alan P. (1992).** "The Four Elementary Forms of Sociality: Framework for a Unified Theory of Social Relations." *Psychological Review*, 99(4), 689–723.
Relational Models Theory. Proposes four elementary relationship models — communal sharing, authority ranking, equality matching, and market pricing — as a unifying typology. Useful if the reciprocity/equity obligation category and the communal-need obligation category are better treated as two instances of one coherent framework rather than separate literatures.

### Vulnerability / dependency (care ethics)

**Noddings, Nel (1984).** *Caring: A Feminine Approach to Ethics and Moral Education.* University of California Press.

**Held, Virginia (2006).** *The Ethics of Care: Personal, Political, and Global.* Oxford University Press.
Provides a normative (not just empirical) grounding for need-responsive obligation, useful for pairing with Clark & Mills if the design wants a moral theory underneath the psychological finding, not just the finding itself.

**Kittay, Eva Feder (1999).** *Love's Labor: Essays on Women, Equality, and Dependency.* Routledge.
Most directly relevant to the project's actual themes — specifically about care obligations arising from dependency within intimate and family relationships.

*(A proposed liberal/consequentialist contrast to this care-ethics group — Goodin (1985) on protecting the vulnerable — has been moved to `unverified_sources.md`, since it hasn't been independently confirmed.)*

### General/background (fairness, coercion)

**Rawls, John (1964).** "Legal Obligation and the Duty of Fair Play." In *Law and Philosophy*, S. Hook (ed.), NYU Press.
Cited in connection with contribution-based reciprocity obligations — fairness generated by unequal contribution over time, not a discrete promise.

**Walster, Elaine, Walster, G. William, & Berscheid, Ellen (1978).** *Equity: Theory and Research.* Allyn & Bacon. (Title corrected — earlier draft omitted the colon.)
Equity theory, cited alongside Rawls and Gouldner as a further grounding for reciprocity/fairness-based obligation.

**Wertheimer, Alan (1987).** *Coercion.* Princeton University Press.
Cited for the "baseline relational norm" obligation category — duties not to coerce or violate autonomy that hold independent of any specific relational agreement.

**Note on framing:** several of these (Clark & Mills 1979, Gouldner 1960, Hochschild 1983) are classic, still-actively-cited originals rather than "contemporary" scholarship in the strict sense. If recent (~last 10 years) sources are specifically wanted, Daminger (2019) is the best confirmed fit from this list. (Brake (2010) was also proposed as a recent source but has been moved to `unverified_sources.md` pending confirmation.)

## 2. Grounding for the Fault-Rating Scale

These were surfaced in response to the concern that the original 0–7 fault scale used stacked intensity-adjectives ("minor" → "significant" → "severe") rather than qualitatively distinct categories, which risked compressing discriminability at the top of the scale.

**Shaver, Kelly G. (1985).** *The Attribution of Blame: Causality, Responsibility, and Blameworthiness.* Springer Series in Social Psychology, Springer-Verlag.
Decomposes blame attribution into discrete components — causality, foreseeability, intentionality, and the presence or absence of an excuse or justification — rather than treating blame as a single undifferentiated intensity. True blame (as opposed to mere causal responsibility) requires foreseeability and intentionality to be present *and* no satisfactory excuse or justification to be accepted. This is the primary source for restructuring the fault scale's anchors around qualitatively different explanations for conduct rather than stronger synonyms for the same explanation.

**Malle, Bertram F., & Nelson, Sarah E. (2003).** "Judging Mens Rea: The Tension between Folk Concepts and Legal Concepts of Intentionality." *Behavioral Sciences & the Law*, 21(5), 563–580.
Argues for an empirically grounded approach to intentionality/mens rea concepts in legal judgment, based on how people actually attribute mental states (belief, desire, awareness, intention, skill) rather than settling the question by theoretical argument alone. More directly "folk-judgment" flavored than Shaver, and specifically useful for treating obligation-clarity and intentionality as separate, measurable inputs to a fault judgment — which the vignette design already does structurally (via the `obligation_identified` field and the intentionality manipulation) but had not yet grounded in a named framework.

**Model Penal Code, §2.02 — Culpability levels (purposeful, knowing, reckless, negligent).**
Not an academic citation but a well-established legal framework, cited for its structural logic rather than its exact category labels: each culpability level describes a *different mental state* relative to the conduct and its consequences, not a stronger version of the same mental state. This is the model used to fix the fault scale's top-end compression problem (anchors 6 and 7 both reaching for "serious/severe" language) — replacing intensity language with distinguishable explanations of what the agent knew and intended.

**"Am I the Asshole?" (r/AmITheAsshole) categorical verdict scheme — YTA / NTA / ESH / NAH.**
Not an academic source, but the actual folk instrument the vignettes are stylistically modeled on (including its convention of parenthetical age/gender tags, e.g. "I (24F) am upset with my partner (28M)"). Cited specifically because it includes verdict categories — **ESH** ("everyone sucks here") and **NAH** ("no assholes here") — that the current continuous 0–7 scale cannot represent: joint fault and mutual non-fault, as distinct from one party being more at fault than the other. Flagged as a candidate addition (categorical verdict field, alongside the continuous rating) rather than a replacement for the existing scale.

## 3. Prior Empirical Studies: Gender Bias in AITA-Style Moral Judgment (Reddit and LLM)

These establish what's already known about gender bias in relationship/dispute moral judgment — both from real human judgments on Reddit and from prior controlled LLM studies — and were the basis for scoping this project's contribution as a family-by-family breakdown rather than a replication of an aggregate yes/no finding.

### Real-world Reddit data

**Betti, L., Bajardi, P., & De Francisci Morales, G. (2025).** "Moral Judgments in Online Discourse are not Biased by Gender." *Scientific Reports*, 15, 21555. (Preprint: *arXiv:2408.12872*, 2024.)
*Correction note: an earlier draft of this review misattributed this paper to "Contarino, S., et al." — that name does not appear anywhere in the actual paper or its citation records and was an error, now corrected. Verified directly against the published Scientific Reports article and its author list (Lorenzo Betti, Paolo Bajardi, Gianmarco De Francisci Morales).*
Uses machine-learning-based propensity score matching on r/AmITheAsshole posts to causally isolate the effect of a protagonist's self-disclosed gender from the effect of the *situation* they're describing — since gender and situation-type are otherwise confounded (different genders tend to post about different kinds of disputes). Finds <cite index="23-1">most of the apparent effect that makes male protagonists more likely to receive a negative judgment disappears when controlling for the situation described in the post</cite>, and overall <cite index="21-1">no direct causal effect of the protagonist's gender on the received moral judgments, except for stories about "friendship and relationships," where male protagonists receive more negative judgments</cite>. This is the real-world correlational/causal baseline: null in general, with the one carved-out exception landing directly in the domain this project studies.

### Prior controlled LLM benchmarks

**Bajaj, D., Lei, Y., Tong, J., & Huang, R. (2024).** "Evaluating Gender Bias of LLMs in Making Morality Judgements." *Findings of EMNLP 2024* / *arXiv:2410.09992*.
Introduces GenMO, a dataset of parallel short stories with male and female characters, and tests GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-3.5-turbo-instruct, GPT-4-turbo, Llama 3/3.1 (8B/70B), Mistral-7B, and Claude 3 (Sonnet, Opus). Finds that <cite index="30-1">despite employing safety checks, all production-standard models tested display significant gender bias, with GPT-3.5-turbo giving biased opinions in 24% of the samples</cite>, and that models <cite index="31-1">exhibit differing moral opinions when the main character's gender is swapped, with a consistent inclination toward female characters up to 88% of the time</cite>. Notably, <cite index="32-1">Claude-3 Sonnet prefers the female protagonist 71.42% of the time while Claude-3 Opus inclines toward the female gender in 63.46% of cases</cite> — directly relevant given this project's own model roster. This is a general moral-judgment benchmark, not specific to relationship/dispute framing, and doesn't break results down by norm-violation category.

**Si, G., Wang, D., Li, Z., Yu, Y., Pan, P., & Zhu, W. (2026).** "Harsher on Male? Evaluating LLMs on Gender-Asymmetric Moral Framing Across Diverse Conflict Scenarios." *arXiv:2606.14068*.
Introduces GAMA-Bench, a gender-mirrored benchmark of 1,298 scenarios spanning intimate-relationship and public-conflict tracks, evaluated across 10 current models (GPT-5.2/5.4, Gemini-2.5/3-Pro, Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro, MiniMax-M2.7, Qwen3/3.5, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, Kimi-2.5). Rather than a single verdict label, it measures six response-framing metrics — punitive wording, therapeutic wording, severity rating, empathy toward the actor, instructional/accusatory content, and full-blame attribution — extracted from open-ended responses by an evaluator model. On the Intimate Track, finds a <cite index="40-1">consistent male-disadvantaging asymmetry: male actors receive more punitive, escalatory, and blame-centered framing, whereas female actors receive more therapeutic and empathy-oriented framing for the same misconduct</cite>, with the pattern persisting <cite index="40-1">across model families, scenario tracks, model scale, and explicit thinking-style reasoning</cite>. The paper also reports that <cite index="40-1">extended reasoning fails to correct the asymmetry — models with chain-of-thought capabilities do not systematically reduce the gaps and sometimes widen them</cite>, and that the asymmetry direction is stable when the evaluator model is swapped and validated against human annotators. This is the closest existing study to the current project's design in spirit (matched gender-mirrored scenarios, structured multi-dimensional scoring rather than a single verdict), though it evaluates response *framing* in open-ended advice-style answers rather than a discrete fault rating, and does not decompose by discrete relationship-norm family (chores, childcare, money, jealousy, etc.) the way this project's 9-family design does.

**Note on dates:** the GAMA-Bench paper (Si et al., 2026) was published after this project's original literature scan; it substantially narrows the "gap" that motivated this project's framing, since it already reports a gender asymmetry that persists across model scale and reasoning mode in relationship-conflict scenarios specifically. It does not, however, break the effect down by *type* of norm violation (the household-labor/childcare/jealousy/etc. taxonomy this project uses), so the family-level decomposition is still not directly superseded — but this paper should be read closely and cited as the most directly competing/complementary prior work, not folded in as background alongside the older Bajaj et al. and Reddit studies.

### Summary of what's already established vs. open

- **Real-world Reddit data:** gender effect is null in general, present specifically in relationship/friendship disputes (Betti et al.).
- **General moral-judgment LLM benchmarks:** models show significant gender bias overall, generally favoring female characters, including specifically in Claude 3 models (Bajaj et al.).
- **Relationship/conflict-specific LLM benchmark:** models show a consistent asymmetry disadvantaging male actors in framing, robust across model scale, reasoning mode, and evaluator choice (Si et al., 2026) — though measured via response framing in open-ended advice answers, not a discrete per-norm fault rating.
- **Still open:** whether the direction and magnitude of gender asymmetry varies *by which relationship norm is violated* (household labor vs. childcare vs. financial provision vs. jealousy vs. emotional labor, etc.) — none of the three studies above decompose by norm-violation type. This remains the project's stated point of contribution, though the Si et al. (2026) paper needs to be directly engaged with and distinguished, not just cited as prior background, given how close its design and findings are.

## 4. LLM-as-Judge Reliability and Validity

Grounds the *instrument* rather than the phenomenon: before a gender effect on the fault-rating DV can be interpreted as meaningful, there needs to be a basis for treating an LLM's numeric rating as a minimally consistent, valid measurement in the first place. This is a precondition for interpreting every other finding in the study.

**Zheng, Lianmin, et al. (2023).** "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena." *NeurIPS 2023* / *arXiv:2306.05685*.
The foundational paper establishing the LLM-as-judge paradigm and its known failure modes. Documents three systematic biases: <cite index="42-1">position bias, verbosity bias, self-enhancement bias, and limited reasoning ability</cite> — specifically, <cite index="44-1">verbosity bias is when an LLM judge favors longer, verbose responses, even if they are not as clear, high-quality, or accurate as shorter alternatives</cite>, demonstrated via a "repetitive list" attack. Despite these biases, the paper finds <cite index="45-1">strong LLM judges like GPT-4 can match both controlled and crowdsourced human preferences well, achieving over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans</cite>. Directly relevant to this project's prompt design: verbosity bias is a live concern for the reasoning/paraphrase ablation work, and self-enhancement bias matters if any model in the roster is ever used to judge its own or a closely related model's output.

**Stureborg, R., et al. (2024) / Haldar, R. & Hockenmaier, J. (2025)**, as summarized in Sec. 2.2 of the "Reliability without Validity" survey below.
Test-retest studies on temperature sensitivity, finding <cite index="55-1">same-verdict rates are above 95% when temperature is set to 0, but fall to as low as 70% when temperature is increased to 1</cite>. Directly actionable for this project's own stability-pass design: confirms that repeated-sampling-based confidence estimation (rather than self-reported confidence) needs enough samples at the actual temperature setting used, since consistency degrades substantially and non-uniformly as temperature rises.

**(Author/venue not yet fully resolved — 2026 preprint).** "Reliability without Validity: A Systematic, Large-Scale Evaluation of LLM-as-a-Judge Models Across Agreement, Consistency, and Bias." *arXiv:2606.19544.*
The most comprehensive and most recent large-scale audit found — 21 judges from nine providers evaluated across three established benchmarks (MT-Bench, JudgeBench, RewardBench). Its title captures a key risk directly relevant to your fault-rating DV: a judge can be internally *consistent* (same input → same output, repeatedly) without that consistency reflecting anything *valid* about the construct being measured. The paper recommends <cite index="53-1">a formal variance-component decomposition that treats runs, items, and position orderings as random effects</cite> as the principled way to plan sample size and replicate counts for judge-validation work — directly relevant to designing this project's stability/replication pass. Needs a full citation pass before this goes in a formal write-up; author list not yet confirmed from search results.

**"Can You Trust LLM Judgments? Reliability of LLM-as-a-Judge."** *arXiv:2412.12509.*
Finds that <cite index="57-1">the ideal temperature for reliable LLM judgment is not universal; it varies depending on the specific LLM and the task</cite>, with different models showing opposite reliability-vs-temperature trends on different tasks. Relevant for justifying whatever temperature setting this study's protocol ultimately uses — the choice needs task-specific justification, not a default assumption that "low temperature is always more reliable" transfers cleanly from other domains.

**Practical implication for this study's design:** the stability-pass / repeated-sampling approach to measuring confidence (discussed earlier in this project, as an alternative to self-reported confidence) is well-grounded in this literature, but the specific temperature and sample-count choices should be justified against the temperature-sensitivity findings above rather than assumed. Worth deciding explicitly, before running the main experiment, how many repeated samples per vignette-cell are needed at the study's chosen temperature to get a stable estimate — this is now an answerable question given the "Reliability without Validity" paper's methodological recommendation, not a judgment call made from scratch.

## 5. Contamination and Memorization: AITA-Style Content in Pretraining Data

Determines whether findings can be interpreted as "the model reasons this way" versus "the model is pattern-matching memorized Reddit judgments" — a validity threat affecting the interpretation of every other result in the study. Directly informs whether this project's vignettes should be written from scratch (as currently planned) rather than adapted from real posts, and whether a manipulation/novelty check is worth adding.

**Russo, G., Nozza, D., Röttger, P., & Hovy, D. (2025).** "The Pluralistic Moral Gap: Understanding Judgment and Value Differences between Humans and Large Language Models." *arXiv:2507.17216.*
The single closest methodological precedent found for this project's contamination concern, because it faces the identical problem: building an LLM moral-judgment study using material sourced from r/AmITheAsshole. States plainly that <cite index="59-1">since LLMs are extensively trained on Reddit—including r/AmITheAsshole—there is a risk that their evaluations reflect memorized patterns rather than moral reasoning</cite>, citing Lesci et al. (2024) and Stoehr et al. on memorization more generally. Their mitigation is a two-step pipeline directly worth adapting: (1) reformulation — rephrase each real dilemma into <cite index="59-1">an abstracted retelling that preserves key narrative elements while removing AITA-specific language and structure</cite>, formatted as a standalone dilemma resembling an everyday moral inquiry; (2) filtering — check whether the model can still identify the rewritten dilemma as AITA-sourced (prompting three times and retaining only posts never flagged), which in their case kept just 7.2% of reformulated posts. This project's vignettes are already written from scratch rather than adapted from real posts, which is a stronger starting position than Russo et al.'s (they start from real posts and have to launder out the AITA fingerprint) — but the same *verification* logic applies: it would be worth running an analogous check, prompting a model to guess whether a given vignette is real vs. synthetic/AITA-derived, as a manipulation check confirming the vignettes don't read as recognizable/memorized material.

**On temperature and model roster, incidentally relevant beyond contamination:** this paper's baseline comparison table reports Claude models' alignment with human moral judgment distributions at both T=0 and T=1, finding performance is better at T=1 for most models tested (including Claude Haiku 3.5, Opus 3, and Sonnet 3.5) — a data point relevant to the temperature-setting discussion in Section 4 above, though for a different task (distributional alignment, not fault rating) and an older model generation.

**General data-contamination-detection literature (supporting/background, not AITA-specific):**

- **Xu, R., et al. (2024).** Cited via secondary sources as establishing a taxonomy of benchmark data contamination severities, from mere exposure to benchmark metadata up to full exposure to benchmark data with labels — useful vocabulary for precisely characterizing what kind of contamination risk this project faces (likely closer to "structural/genre familiarity" than "verbatim memorized instance," given vignettes are written from scratch).
- **Yang, S., Chiang, W.-L., Zheng, L., Gonzalez, J. E., & Stoica, I. (2023).** "Rethinking Benchmark and Contamination for Language Models with Rephrased Samples." *arXiv:2311.04850.*
  Establishes that simple n-gram-overlap contamination detection can miss contamination that survives rephrasing — relevant caution for interpreting any novelty/manipulation check this project runs, since a model failing to identify a rewritten vignette as "AITA-like" doesn't fully rule out subtler structural contamination.
- **Carlini, N., et al. (2023).** Cited via secondary sources as demonstrating that memorization increases with model size, data frequency, and sufficient context — relevant background for why larger/more capable models in this project's roster might show *more* pattern-matching risk on AITA-genre material, not less, all else equal.

**Practical implication for this study's design:** the current plan (write vignettes from scratch, in a fixed template, rather than adapt real AITA posts) already avoids the primary contamination risk Russo et al. had to engineer around. The residual risk is *structural* familiarity — the AITA genre conventions themselves (parenthetical age/gender tags, "Was I the asshole?" framing, first-person dispute-narration structure) are heavily represented in pretraining data even when the specific scenario is invented. Worth considering, as a cheap addition: a small manipulation check asking a separate model call whether a given vignette "sounds like" a real AITA post, as a genre-familiarity control — not to eliminate the genre resemblance (which, per the earlier ecological-validity discussion, is actually desirable for deployment-relevance), but to have a documented answer to the question of how recognizable the material is, rather than an assumption either way.

## 6. Ambivalent Sexism / Chivalry-Paternalism Theory: Explaining Direction-Flips

Grounds *why* gender-attribution effects might flip direction depending on context — needed for the discussion section once the family-by-family results come in, given the existing literature already shows contradictory directions (female-favoring in general moral judgment per Bajaj et al.; male-disadvantaging in relationship-conflict framing per Si et al. 2026; null except relationships in real Reddit data per Betti et al.).

**Glick, P., & Fiske, S. T. (1996).** "The Ambivalent Sexism Inventory: Differentiating Hostile and Benevolent Sexism." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 70(3), 491–512.
The foundational paper. Presents <cite index="71-1">a theory of sexism formulated as ambivalence toward women</cite>, distinguishing <cite index="71-1">sexist antipathy or Hostile Sexism (HS) and a subjectively positive (for sexist men) orientation toward women, Benevolent Sexism (BS)</cite>. Critically, <cite index="72-1">benevolent sexism is based on patronizing attitudes and behaviours that are often seen as positive by men and women — yet reinforce women's subordinate status</cite>, while hostile sexism is reserved for <cite index="72-1">women who do not adhere to the traditional gender hierarchy</cite>. This is the theoretical resource for explaining *why* a model might rate a female agent's fault lower in some contexts specifically because she's being cast in a protected, "needs shielding" role (paternalism) rather than because of any general leniency — a mechanistically different claim than "the model just likes women more."

**Glick, P., & Fiske, S. T. (1999).** "The Ambivalence Toward Men Inventory: Differentiating Hostile and Benevolent Beliefs about Men." *Psychology of Women Quarterly*, 23(3), 519–536.
The companion measure, and arguably more directly relevant to this project's own findings than the ASI, since your literature review already shows a *male-disadvantaging* pattern in relationship-conflict contexts (Si et al., 2026). The AMI's <cite index="79-1">Hostility toward Men (HM) and Benevolence toward Men (BM) subscales tap conventional attitudes toward men that have opposing valences</cite> — hostility toward men centers on <cite index="78-1">resentment of paternalism, compensatory gender differentiation, and heterosexual hostility</cite> (i.e., men as unearned holders of power/control, deserving of blame and correction), while benevolence toward men centers on <cite index="78-1">maternalism, complementary gender differentiation, and heterosexual intimacy</cite> (i.e., men as needing to be managed, guided, or civilized by women). The "Resentment of Paternalism" subscale content is a strong candidate mechanism for a male-disadvantaging pattern in exactly the domains this project studies (control, financial provision, household authority): a model could be tracking a cultural script that a man's failure to meet an obligation reflects poorly on him *because men are expected to hold up their end of a power/provider role*, which is a different mechanism than simple anti-male animus.

**Note on directionality and prediction, not just post-hoc explanation:** taken together, ASI and AMI give a specific, falsifiable prediction to test against the family-by-family results, not just a retrospective story. Benevolent sexism toward women (protective paternalism) predicts female-favoring leniency concentrated in families where the norm violation could be framed as her needing protection or accommodation (e.g., emotional labor, sexual expectations). Hostility toward men via resentment-of-paternalism predicts male-disadvantaging harshness concentrated in families involving power, control, or provider-role failure (e.g., financial provision, household authority, jealousy/control). If the family-by-family breakdown instead shows the *same* direction and magnitude uniformly across all 9 families, that would argue against an ambivalent-sexism account and toward a simpler, undifferentiated leniency/harshness bias — a useful contrast to build into the analysis plan now, before the data come in, rather than fitting an explanation after the fact.

**Prior application to LLM bias, for precedent (not yet applied to relationship fault-attribution specifically):**
- A 2026 FAccT paper on gender bias scoring in LLM-generated text cites Glick & Fiske (1996) directly as a framework for identifying benevolent-vs-hostile bias patterns in model output — precedent for using ASI/AMI categories as an analysis lens on LLM text, not just human survey responses.
- A study on LLM-generated synthetic personas found <cite index="87-1">a significant association between the female gender and the values of universalism and benevolence</cite> in model outputs, explicitly characterizing this as <cite index="87-1">a genuine expression of the benevolent sexism described by Glick and Fiske (1996)</cite> — direct evidence that ambivalent-sexism patterns show up in generative model behavior, not just classification/scoring tasks, which is closer to what your fault-rating DV measures.
- None of the sources found apply ASI/AMI specifically to relationship-dispute fault attribution or an AITA-style task — this remains open territory for your project's discussion section to occupy, rather than something already claimed by prior work.

## 7. Gender Fault-Attribution Bias in Intimate/Relationship Contexts (Marital Attribution Literature)

Narrower and more mechanistically relevant to this project's actual vignette content than general moral-judgment bias literature: this is the psych literature closest to your setup — a partner judging a partner's fault for a specific behavior within an ongoing relationship — rather than a bystander/third-party judging a stranger.

**Bradbury, T. N., & Fincham, F. D. (1990).** "Attributions in Marriage: Review and Critique." *Psychological Bulletin*, 107(1), 3–33.
The foundational review establishing marital attribution theory. Distinguishes three related but conceptually separate judgments partners make about each other's negative behavior: **causal attribution** (locus, stability, globality — who/what caused it, and how enduring/general that cause is), **responsibility attribution** (intent, motivation, blameworthiness), and **blame** (an evaluative judgment layered on top of responsibility). Central finding: <cite index="100-1">dissatisfied spouses, compared with satisfied spouses, make attributions for the partner's behavior that cast it in a negative light</cite> — specifically, distressed partners attribute negative behavior to <cite index="100-1">enduring characteristics</cite> of the partner rather than to the specific situation. This three-way causal/responsibility/blame distinction is structurally close to what your fault-rating scale is already trying to capture (via `obligation_identified`, the intentionality manipulation, and the fault rating itself) — worth explicitly citing as the psychological-construct precedent for why your DV is decomposed the way it is, separate from Shaver's more general blame-attribution model already in Section 2 of this review.

**Fincham, F. D., & Bradbury, T. N. (1992).** "Assessing Attributions in Marriage: The Relationship Attribution Measure." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 62(3), 457–468.
Introduces the Relationship Attribution Measure (RAM), the standard instrument for eliciting these attributions from real partners about real relationship events. Explicitly distinguishes <cite index="98-1">causal attributions [that] establish who or what caused an event</cite> from <cite index="98-1">responsibility attributions [that] establish accountability for an event</cite>, with <cite index="98-1">blame attributions constitut[ing] evaluative judgments</cite> layered on top of both. Useful as a template if you ever want to validate your own fault-rating instrument against an established human measure, or want precise vocabulary for describing what your `obligation_identified` field and fault rating are each doing.

Together, these establish that attributions aren't just descriptive but predictive: partners who make <cite index="97-1">nonbenign attributions for negative partner behavior</cite> show lower marital satisfaction a year later, an effect that <cite index="97-1">was not due to depression, self-esteem, or initial level of marital satisfaction</cite>. Relevant mainly as a reminder that in the real psychological literature, fault attribution in relationships is not a neutral, static judgment — it's causally entangled with relationship trajectory. Not directly load-bearing for this project's design, but useful context for why the topic matters beyond LLM-bias measurement per se.

**On whether this literature shows a *gender* asymmetry specifically:** the search here surfaces a genuine gap rather than a clean answer. Some individual studies report gender-differentiated patterns in attribution *style* — one older study found <cite index="106-1">wives were more likely to provide attributions indicating equal responsibility to both partners while the husbands provided attributions indicating responsibility to self or other</cite>, suggesting husbands attribute in more self-serving ways than wives — but this describes how husbands and wives attribute blame to *each other*, not how a third-party observer (or an LLM) attributes blame to a male vs. female target holding the situation fixed. That's a different question from your study's design, and the marital-attribution literature searched here doesn't appear to directly answer your specific question (whether an observer's fault judgment shifts based on the target's gender, situation held constant). A separate, adjacent literature on domestic-violence blame attribution does show a clearer third-party gender asymmetry — one study found <cite index="102-1">males and females attributed less blame and behavioral stability to female perpetrators than male perpetrators, especially if the perpetrator was provoked</cite> — which is closer in structure to your design (observer judging a gendered target) but concerns a much more severe, distinct category of behavior (physical violence) than most of your 9 norm families, so it should be cited cautiously as a directional precedent rather than a close analogue.

**Honest assessment for this section:** the classic marital-attribution literature (Bradbury & Fincham and the RAM) is valuable for construct vocabulary and for validating that your DV's structure (causal/responsibility/blame decomposition) mirrors an established psychological framework — but it does not, on this search, supply a direct "gender bias in third-party fault attribution within relationship disputes" finding the way the AITA-specific and LLM-specific literatures in Sections 1–3 do. The domestic-violence blame literature is the closest partial substitute found, with the caveat about severity/category mismatch noted above. This is worth flagging honestly rather than overstating: if a tighter match exists, it likely sits in social-psychology work on third-party observers judging vignette-based relationship conflicts specifically (as opposed to the classic Fincham/Bradbury paradigm, which studies the two partners judging each other) — worth a follow-up search on "observer attribution third party relationship conflict vignette gender" if this gap matters for the write-up.
